SUMMARY
The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) can generate robust circadian behaviors in mammals under different en-
vironments, but the underlying neural mechanisms remained unclear. Here, we showed that the activities of
cholecystokinin (CCK) neurons in the mouse SCN preceded the onset of behavioral activities under different
photoperiods. CCK-neuron-deﬁcient mice displayed shortened free-running periods, failed to compress
their activities under a long photoperiod, and developed rapid splitting or became arrhythmic under constant
light. Furthermore, unlike vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) neurons, CCK neurons are not directly light
sensitive, but their activation can elicit phase advance and counter light-induced phase delay mediated by
VIP neurons. Under long photoperiods, the impact of CCK neurons on SCN dominates over that of VIP neu-
rons. Finally, we found that the slow-responding CCK neurons control the rate of recovery during jet lag.
Together, our results demonstrated that SCN CCK neurons are crucial for the robustness and plasticity of
the mammalian circadian clock.
INTRODUCTION
The endogenous circadian clock drives daily oscillations of a
wide range of physiological processes and behaviors in diverse
organisms. At the molecular level, circadian rhythm is generated
by the negative transcription-translation feedback loops (TTFLs)
consisting of core clock genes such as Per1/2/3, Cry1/2, Bmal1,
and Clock in mammals.1 Although most individual cells possess
the circadian clock, the overt circadian activities of animals are
entrained by the external environmental cues. Light has been
considered the most important environmental factor inﬂuencing
circadian rhythm. The circadian clock is entrained by 24-h light-
dark cycles but continues to function under seasonal changes in
environment. Thus, the robustness and plasticity of circadian
clocks is crucial for animal survival. The circadian activities in
most animals show a bimodal distribution, with two activity
bouts aligned to the dawn and dusk, respectively.2 The seasonal
adaptation of circadian behaviors is manifested by the adjust-
ment of these two activity bouts under long and short photope-
riods. Under the prolonged period of constant light (LL), animal
activity can gradually split into two free-running components.
Based on these observations, Pittendrigh and Daan proposed
that two separate but coupled oscillators, namely the evening
(E) and morning (M) oscillators, control these two activity bouts,
respectively.3 This model predicts that E and M oscillators have
different free-running periods, show different responses to light
stimulus, and that their phase difference changes according to
the day’s length, thus providing a basis for seasonal encoding
of the circadian clock. In Drosophila, E and M oscillators have
been identiﬁed as the speciﬁc subsets of clock neurons each
of which can dominate the circadian system under different
light-dark conditions.4,5 However, it is still unclear whether sim-
ple E and M oscillators or a network of coupled oscillators are
responsible for driving the E and M circadian behaviors and con-
trolling the seasonal plasticity in mammals.6
The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), consisting of 20,000
cells,has beenknown as the master circadian pacemaker inmam-
mals.7 Light information enters the SCN from intrinsically photo-
sensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs), and the inter-cellular
coupling within the SCN is critical for generating synchronized os-
cillations to govern downstream behaviors.8 Past studies of the
SCN have mainly focused on neuropeptide-releasing neurons,
including vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) and gastrin-
releasing peptide (GRP) neurons in the ventral and core regions

shell region of the SCN. It was suggested that VIP neurons receive
direct retinal input and are important for phase resetting in the light
entrainment pathway and synchronization of cellular circadian
clocks,9–12 while AVP neurons have also been shown to be impor-
tant for inter-cellular coupling within the SCN network.13–15 Neuro-

been implicated in the seasonal plasticity of the SCN.17–21 As
almost all SCN neurons are GABAergic, it is also unclear which
subtypes of SCN neurons contribute to the seasonal plasticity of
the circadian clock. In previous studies of the SCN on brain slices,
Per2 luciferase expression of neurons located at different SCN
subregions has been shown to oscillate at different phases.17,22
The exposure to long-day photoperiods and LL can increase their
phase dispersion and weaken inter-cellular coupling among SCN
neurons.23,24 However, the identities of these differentially phased
cells and the underlying mechanism remained unknown. Our
recent study using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and
tissue-cleared imaging has suggested that SCN neurons can be
divided into Avp+/Nms+, Vip+/Nms+, Cck+/C1ql3+, Grp+/Vip+,
and Cck+/Bdnf+ subtypes.25 These neuronal subtypes display
distinct circadian gene expression and light responsiveness and
occupy different spatial locations in the SCN. Compared with
VIP and AVP neurons, cholecystokinin (CCK) neurons showed a
delayed phase of circadian clock gene expression. Furthermore,
CCK neurons are enriched in the anterior shell region of the SCN,
where E clock cells were thought to be located.22 So far, the circa-
dian functions of CCK neurons in the SCN are still unclear.
In this study, by monitoring in vivo calcium activity and behav-
ioral activities in free-moving animals, we found that the calcium
activities of SCN CCK neurons peaked before the activity onset
under different photoperiods, while those of VIP neurons were
spread over the entire light phase under a long-day photoperiod.
Under LL, the calcium activities of CCK neurons remained oscil-
lating, while those of VIP neurons lost rhythmicity. CCK neuron
deﬁciency led to the shortening of the free-running period and
the instability of circadian behaviors—speciﬁcally under long-
day photoperiods and LL. Furthermore, we found that although
CCK neurons are not directly light sensitive, the activation of
CCK neurons can phase-advance the circadian clock and
counter the phase delaying effect of light, thereby stabilizing
the circadian clock under long-day photoperiods. The impact
of VIP neurons on the SCN network was signiﬁcantly weakened,
while that of CCK neurons was strengthened in long-day photo-
periods. Finally, we found that the response of CCK neuronal ac-
tivity to the sudden change in the light-dark cycle is signiﬁcantly
slower than that of VIP neurons, and the inhibition of CCK neu-
rons can signiﬁcantly shorten the time of recovery in jet lag. In
summary, we revealed that SCN CCK neurons confer the robust-
ness and plasticity needed for the circadian clock in mice.
RESULTS
Activities of SCN CCK neurons track the onset of
circadian behavioral activities
We ﬁrst examined the spontaneous calcium activities of SCN
CCK and VIP neurons by long-term in vivo ﬁber photometry in
free-moving CCK-Cre and VIP-Cre mice infected by adeno-
associated virus (AAV) vectors encoding GCaMP6s (AAV-
EF1a-DIO-GCaMP6s-P2A-nls-tdTomato) while monitoring their
locomotion and body temperature (Figure 1A). The tdTomato+
cells in CCK-Cre mice were mainly located in the shell region
of anterior SCN and those of VIP-Cre mice in the ventral region
of the posterior SCN, respectively, consistent with the speciﬁc
spatial locations of CCK and VIP neurons reported in the
SCN25 (Figures 1B and S1A). Under 12L:12D (light-dark cycle)
condition, we found that SCN CCK neurons exhibited in vivo
circadian rhythm peaking at ZT7.05 ± 0.37 (Zeitgeber time) in
spontaneous calcium activity, about 5 h before the onset of loco-
motion and the rise in body temperature at light-off (ZT12)
(Figures 1C, 1D, and 1I). In comparison, SCN VIP neurons
showed high levels of calcium activity across the entire span of
daytime, consistent with the previous study.26 The waveforms
of calcium activities are signiﬁcantly different between CCK
and VIP neurons (p = 2.25 3 105, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test).
We measured the half widths of their calcium peaks in both rising
(W1) and declining (W2) phases, and found that the calcium pro-
ﬁle of CCK neurons is narrower in terms of total half-width (W1 +
W2) and more asymmetric in terms of W1/W2 than the calcium
proﬁle of VIP neurons (Figures 1J and 1K). VIP neurons peaked
at around ZT6.06 ± 0.29, earlier than the peak time of CCK neu-
rons (Figure 1I). The ﬁber locations and the numbers of infected
CCK neurons did not signiﬁcantly affect the daily calcium proﬁles
for SCN CCK neurons (Figures S1A and S1B). Upon release from
12L:12D to constant dark (DD) conditions, the circadian calcium
activities of CCK and VIP neurons free run together with the loco-
motion and body temperature rhythms, during which time the
differential proﬁles of CCK and VIP neuron calcium activities
were maintained (Figures 1C, 1E, and 1M).
A long-day photoperiod is known to increase the phase
dispersion among SCN neurons and the release into DD will
lead to resynchronization.17,21 We next measured the calcium
activities in CCK and VIP neurons under long-day photoperiods
(20L:4D) (Figures 1C, 1F, and S1C). Under 20L:4D, VIP neuron
calcium activity peaked at ZT2.42 ± 0.25 (light-off deﬁned as
ZT12) again at the middle point of activity onset and offset, re-
sulting in a signiﬁcant increase of phase difference relative to ac-
tivity onset (D phase-onset; Figures 1I, 1F, and S2A–S2D). The
daily calcium activity proﬁle of VIP neurons became signiﬁcantly
broadened in the daytime and showed a decreased relative
amplitude (RA) of oscillation and an increased baseline level un-
der a long-day photoperiod (Figures 1L and S2E). Speciﬁcally,
VIP neurons showed symmetric extension of calcium activity un-
der 20L:4D, with increased total half-width compared with
12L:12D (Figures 1J, S2F, and S2G). In CCK neurons, the RA
of oscillation in calcium activity also decreased, but to a much
lesser degree, and the baseline level of calcium activity was
not signiﬁcantly different in 20L:4D compared with 12L:12D
(Figures 1L and S2H). Under 20L:4D conditions, the calcium ac-
tivity of CCK neurons slowly increased in the ﬁrst half of day-time
period and reached the peak at ZT7.33 ± 0.49, still around 5 h
before the onset of locomotion activity and the rise of body tem-
perature (Figures 1C, 1G, 1M, S2C, and S2D). CCK neurons
showed increased asymmetry of calcium activity (W1/W2) under
20L:4D (Figure 1K), with an increased W1 but unchanged W2

We then examined the wheel running activities of CCK-Casp3
mice under 20L:4D. Under such long-day conditions, the activity

CCK neurons are necessary for circadian activity under
constant light
We measured the in vivo calcium activities of VIP and CCK neu-
rons in the SCN, together with locomotor activity and body tem-
perature in animals under LL (Figure 3A). We found that the cal-
cium rhythm of VIP neurons was rapidly dampened under LL, as
indicated by decreased RA and broadened peak width, consis-
tent with previous reports26 (Figure 3B). However, the RA of cal-
cium rhythms in CCK neurons also decreased, but reached a
stable level, and the peak width of CCK neurons remained con-
stant upon entry into LL (Figure 3B). The calcium activity of CCK
neurons exhibited circadian rhythm free-running with a period
longer than 24 h. The peak of calcium activity of CCK neurons
was again phase-locked to the onset of locomotor activity and
the rise of body temperature in mice under LL (Figures 3C and
3D). We found that the rhythmicity of CCK neuron calcium activ-
ities remained stable, while the rhythmicity of VIP neurons
decreased from 12:12D to 20L:4D and was lost in LL (Figure 3E).
We next tested the wheel running activities of CCK-neuron-
deﬁcient mice under LL. We found that 26.3% of CCK-Casp3
mice developed behavioral splitting, during which a short period
component (22.68 ± 0.12 h) emerged from the long period
component (24.41 ± 0.33 h) within an average of 4.8 days under
LL (Figures 3F, S5A, and S5C; Data S1). Furthermore, 36.8% of
CCK-Casp3 mice became arrhythmic immediately upon being
transferred to LL from 12L:12D (Figures 3F, 3G, and 3H; Data
S1). In contrast, the running wheel activities of control mice never
split during the course of the experiment and only one animal
became arrhythmic under LL (Figures 3G and S5B). Similarly,
we found that the wheel running activities of 50% of CCK-
TeNT mice rapidly became arrhythmic under LL conditions
(Figures 3I–3K, S5E, and S5F; Data S1). Therefore, our results
suggested that CCK neurons remained rhythmic and were
crucial for maintaining circadian behavioral activities under LL
conditions.

DISCUSSION
In this study, we investigated the functions of mouse SCN CCK
neurons in the circadian clock. We showed that the calcium ac-
tivity of SCN CCK neurons tracked the E behavioral activity onset
and that the dysfunction of CCK neurons signiﬁcantly impaired
circadian time keeping under long-day photoperiods and LL.
We further demonstrated that the activation of these CCK neu-
rons induced phase advances under different photoperiods
and that CCK neurons antagonized VIP neurons in the light
entrainment pathway. We found that the activation of CCK neu-
rons by VIP neurons was diminished and that CCK neurons
began activating rather than inhibiting VIP neurons under long-
day photoperiods. Finally, we suggested that the slow adapta-
tion of SCN CCK neurons under the sudden shift of day-night
cycles underlies the phenomenon of jet lag. Thus, CCK neurons
and their interactions with other SCN neurons may provide a
neural basis for the robustness and plasticity of the circadian
clock in mammals.
Heterogeneous circadian functions of SCN neuronal
subtypes
As the central mammalian circadian clock, SCN contains many
different neuronal subtypes. For many of these subtypes, their
roles in daily timekeeping remain to be systematically examined.
In this study, we have mainly investigated the functions of CCK
neurons in contrast with VIP neurons. Our previous study re-
vealed that VIP and CCK neurons can each be divided into
further subtypes. VIP neurons consist of the Vip+/Nms+ subtype,
with strong circadian rhythmicity, and Grp+/Vip+, with the stron-
gest light responsiveness. Thus, the calcium activities of VIP
neurons that we measured in this study can represent the com-
posite activities of Vip+/Nms+ and Grp+/Vip+ subtypes. Similarly,
CCK neurons consist of Cck+/C1ql3+ and Cck+/Bdnf+ subtypes.
According to our scRNA-seq data, Cck+/C1ql3+ neurons consti-
tute the majority of CCK neurons and may give rise to the rhyth-
micity of calcium activity, with a delayed phase relative to other
SCN neuronal subtypes. Interestingly, brain-derived neurotro-
phic factor (BDNF) has been previously reported to regulate
the phase shift by light pulse.29 Thus, the Cck+/Bdnf+ subtype
could be responsible for the effects of CCK neurons in the light
entrainment pathway that we observed in this study.
AVP neurons and AVP neuropeptide signaling have also been
suggested to play important roles in the SCN.13–15 When the
intrinsic clocks or GABA transmission in AVP neurons were
compromised, E and M activity peaks started to disasso-
ciate—even under 12L:12D condition.13,30,31 It was reported
that the calcium activities in AVP neurons peak at dawn,30

The SCN neural network and its plasticity
The SCN neural network of heterogeneous neuronal subtypes is
responsible for converting light information into rhythmic behav-
iors. In this network, SCN VIP neurons are believed to be crucial
for the light entrainment of circadian clocks.10,26,32 The calcium
activities of VIP neurons were light responsive, as shown in this
study and previous studies.12,26 In contrast, we found that the
light pulse in early subjective night did not immediately elevate
the calcium activities in CCK neurons. However, direct optoge-
netic stimulation of VIP neurons can activate CCK neurons,
and the calcium rhythm of CCK neurons still displayed phase
shift on the day after light pulse. These results indicated that
CCK neurons were downstream of VIP neurons and that the
response of CCK neurons to VIP neurons is likely weak, such
that only artiﬁcial activation of VIP neurons by high-frequency
optogenetic stimulation can activate Fos expression in CCK neu-
rons. Our manipulation of CCK neurons by optogenetics or che-
mogenetics paired with a light pulse affected the phase shift of
circadian behavior, suggesting that slow-responding CCK neu-
rons may be blocking the phase delay of the light pulse by inhib-
iting VIP neuron activation. Although CCK neurons are not light-
activated, it has been suggested that SCN CCK neurons receive
inputs from several brain regions involved in non-photic path-
ways.33 Thus, our study may provide a potential mechanism
for non-photic entrainment of circadian clocks and the interac-
tion between photic and non-photic pathways. The mutual
coupling between VIP and CCK neurons can be mediated by
various neuropeptides, including VIP, CCK, BDNF, and prokine-
ticin 2 (PROK2), based on the expression of the genes coding for
these neuropeptides and their receptors in SCN neuronal sub-
types.25 Previously, Hannibal et al. reported that mice lacking
the CCK gene (CCK-null) exhibit a circadian rhythm phenotype
comparable to that of WT controls.34 In this study, our targeted
deletion of CCK neurons not only abolished CCK gene expres-
sion but also removed all cellular signaling outputs and inputs
mediated by CCK neurons in the SCN network. Thus, the differ-
ences in phenotypes between our CCK-neuron-deleted mice
and CCK-null mice suggested that neuronal mechanisms other
than CCK neuropeptide signaling may be responsible for the
circadian functions of CCK neurons identiﬁed in our study.
In addition to neuropeptide signaling, neurotransmitter GABA
could also be involved in the coupling of SCN neurons. In SCN,
the action of GABA signaling can be both inhibitory and excit-
atory, depending on intra-cellular chloride concentration in the
recipient neurons.21 The negative feedback interaction between
VIP and CCK neurons found in our study is reminiscent of GABA-
mediated asymmetric connectivity between core and shell
subregions of the SCN, proposed previously.28 The differences
in results we obtained for CCK neuron ablation by caspase
versus CCK neuron silencing by TeNT hinted that CCK neurons
may use multiple signals, considering that GABA-mediating
signaling was more abrogated with CCK-TeNT silencing. Finally,
we noted that the active time window of CCK neurons in subjec-
tive day coincides with the ‘‘dead zone’’ of the phase response
curve during which the circadian clock showed no phase shift
by light. Therefore, the negative feedback loop of VIP and CCK
neurons may contribute to the gating of the response of the
circadian clock to external light at speciﬁc times of day.
As the main environmental factor deﬁning seasons, light also

E and M oscillator model
Our study allowed us to shed new light on the E and M oscillator
model proposed by Pittendrigh and Daan in 1976. In their original
model, E and M oscillators tracked E activity onset and M activity
offset, respectively, in nocturnal animals. In our analysis of Fos
expression, AVP neurons seem to track the M-peak and CCK
neurons track the E-peak, with VIP neurons in between. The
phase delay of CCK neurons relative to VIP and AVP neurons
could be due to the longer intrinsic periods of CCK neurons, as
the deletion of CCK neurons shortened the free-running periods
of the animals. Additional mechanisms, such as neuropeptide
signaling, may further contribute to the diversiﬁcation of activity
rhythms across the neuronal subtypes in mammalian pace-
makers, as it does in Drosophila.37,38 The E and M model also
predicted that the E and M oscillators decelerate and accelerate
the clock, respectively. In contrast with VIP neurons, the activa-
tion of CCK neurons speciﬁcally generated phase advance in
late subjective night instead of phase delay in early subjective
night, suggesting that VIP and CCK neurons may account for
the delay and advance portions of the phase response curve,
respectively. Furthermore, the E and M model assumed mutual
coupling between E and M oscillators. Our results suggest a
negative feedback loop between VIP and CCK neurons. Finally,
in the E and M model, the phase relationship between E and M
oscillators changes according to seasons and is accompanied
by changes in their coupling strength. Indeed, we found that
the activation of CCK neurons by VIP neurons decreased, while
the signaling from CCK to VIP neurons switched sign from inhi-
bition
to
excitation
under
long-day
photoperiods.
Taken
together, we found that the mutual coupling of VIP and CCK neu-
rons is largely consistent with the E and M model, but other SCN
neuronal subtypes still need to be included in the model, and
additional experiments are required to substantiate this model
in the future.
Among the clock neurons in Drosophila, the M cells expressing
pigment-dispersing factor (PDF) in small ventral lateral neurons
(sLNv) and the E cells in dorsal lateral neurons (LNd) have been
identiﬁed.4,5 Furthermore, E cells were shown to express a
PDF receptor.39 According to our scRNA-seq data, SCN CCK
neurons also expressed VIPR2, the receptor for VIP, indicating
that the signaling pathway from VIP to CCK neurons in the
SCN resembles the PDF signaling from M cells to E cells in ﬂies.
The phase relationship between the M cells and E cells encodes
for seasonality in Drosophila.40 Similarly, the circadian phases in
both calcium activity and Fos gene expression of VIP, CCK, and
AVP neurons encode the seasonal information in mice, with AVP
and CCK neurons aligning with dawn and dusk, respectively. In
ﬂies, it was proposed that M cells oscillate in DD while E cells
oscillate in LL, with E cells dominating over M cells in long-day
photoperiods.41 SCN CCK neurons continue to function in
long-day photoperiods and LL, suggesting that SCN CCK neu-
rons may function as the tentative E cells in ﬂies. Whether SCN
VIP or AVP neurons function like M cells requires further studies.
In summary, the mammalian circadian system in the SCN, con-
sisting of VIP, CCK, and AVP neurons, appears to share similar-
ities with the clock neuron system in ﬂies, suggesting the neural
mechanisms for seasonal plasticity are potentially conserved
across evolution.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS
Animals
All experiments were conducted with 8-24 week old male mice. All mice were housed at a speciﬁc pathogen-free facility with exhaust
ventilated, close-system, cage rank. At most ﬁve mice were caged together in a corncob-bedded cage. The room was controlled at
20–22C, 30–70% humidity and a 12h light-dark cycle (LD). Animals were fed ad lib, with access to water and standard mouse chow
(Shoobree). For in vivo ﬁber photometry and optogenetics experiments, we used heterozygous CCK-IRES-Cre and VIP-IRES-Cre
mice on a C57BL/6 background that were at least 7 weeks old at the time of virus injection. Following stereotactic injection, ﬁberoptic
cannula implantation and recovery for 1 week, mice were single-housed in custom-built cages (20cm 3 20cm 3 30cm) with ad lib
access to food and water. For optogenetic and chemogenetic experiments, we used heterozygous CCK-IRES-Cre and VIP-IRES-Cre
mice on a C57BL/6 background that were at least 7 weeks old at the time of virus injection. To help identify CCK cells in some
experiments, mice were crossed to Ai9 mice to yield heterozygous CCK-IRES-Cre:Ai9 mice that expressed a tdTomato ﬂuorescent
reporter in CCK cells. All animal experiments conform with the agreement of the Committee on Laboratory Animals of the Institute of
Neuroscience, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IACUC no. NA-023-2022).
METHOD DETAILS
Viruses
To ablate SCN CCK neurons, AAV-CAG-DIO-taCaspase3-TEVp-WPRE-pA (AAV2/9, titer: 5.5 3 1012 v.g./mL, from Taitool Biosci-

DIO-GCaMP6s-P2A-nls-tdTomato (AAV2/9, titer: 8.0 3 1012v.g./mL, from Taitool Bioscience) was injected into SCN unilaterally in
CCK-IRES-Cre and VIP-IRES-Cre mice at a volume of 100nL. To inhibit the neurotransmitter release of SCN CCK neurons, AAV-
hSyn-DIO-TeNT-P2A-EGFP-WPRE-pA (AAV2/9, titer: 2.0 3 1012v.g./mL, from BrainVTA) was injected into SCN bilaterally in
CCK-IRES-Cre at a volume of 80nL for each side. To inhibit the neuron activity of SCN CCK neurons, AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-
EGFP-WPRE-pA (AAV2/9, titer: 5.0 3 1012v.g./mL, from Taitool Bioscience) was injected into SCN bilaterally in CCK-IRES-Cre at
a volume of 80nL for each side. To activate the neuron activity of SCN CCK or VIP neurons, AAV-hEF1a-DIO-ChR2-EYFP-WPRE-
pA (AAV2/9, titer: 9.6 3 1012v.g./mL, from Taitool Bioscience) was injected into SCN bilaterally in CCK-IRES-Cre or VIP-IRES-Cre
at a volume of 40nL for each side.
Virus injection and stereotaxic surgery
All stereotactic surgeries were performed generally on a RWD stereotactic (model 68507, RWD Life Science) frame with animals
anesthetized under isoﬂurane when body temperature of the animal was maintained by a heating pad. Brieﬂy, the skull was exposed
with a small incision and injections of viruses or tracers were carried out via an oil pressure system using glass pipettes (tip diameter
of 10-30mm) connected to the NARISHIGE mo-10 micromanipulator pump at a rate of 20nL/min. For viral injection, the following co-
ordinates, according to the standard mouse brain atlas (Paxinos and Franklin Mouse Brain Atlas, 2nd edition), were used to target
SCN, AP: -0.550 mm, LM: 0.750mm, DV: 5.65mm, dip angle of six degrees. For optogenetics manipulations, unilateral optic ﬁbers
(diameter, 200 mm; N.A., 0.37; length, 7 mm for SCN; AniLab Software and Instruments Co., Ltd) were implanted (SCN, AP:
-0.550mm, LM: 0.200mm, DV: 5.250mm) through the drilled holes and secured onto the skull with dental cement and 3M adhesive.
For ﬁber photometry recordings, unilateral optic ﬁbers (400 mm core; 0.57 NA; 1.5 m in length; Doric Lenses) were implanted (SCN,
AP: -0.550mm, LM: 0.200mm, DV: 5.250mm) through the drilled holes and secured onto the skull with dental cement and 3M
adhesive.

Fiber photometry data analysis
Acquired data ﬁles were loaded and processed with custom-written codes in MATLAB. First, we extracted the calcium-dependent
signal (470nm) and the calcium-independent signal (405nm). Then, the detected signal was averaged in a 30 s session. The 405nm
signal was calculated as the baseline (F). We calculated the changes in ﬂuorescence (DF) as 475nm signal minus 405nm signal. The
changes in ﬂuorescence over baseline (DF/F) were calculated as (475nm signal – 405nm signal)/(405nm signal).
Optogenetic manipulations and behavior rhythm recordings
For in vivo activation of SCN neurons, anesthetized mice were placed into a stereotaxic device and unilaterally implanted optic ﬁbers
(diameter, 200 mm; N.A., 0.37; length, 7 mm for SCN; AniLab Software and Instruments Co., Ltd) through the drilled holes and
secured onto the skull with dental cement and 3M adhesive. After at least 1-week recovery, mice surgically received a telemetry
sensor implant (TA-F10 transmitter, Data Sciences International, New Brighton, MN, USA). The implanting surgery was conducted
as per manufacturer instructions. After at least 1-week recovery, each mouse was individually housed in a cage that was placed on
top of a receiver (DSI). Throughout the experiment, signals were collected at 1 Hz and saved as 1-min consecutive average by a PC
desktop running Ponemah physiology platform 6.5 software (DSI). Tb and LMA values were recorded at 1-min intervals. The cage of
each mouse was changed every 2 weeks, and health checks were performed daily by real-time data analysis. Then mice were
chronically tethered to a ﬁber-optic cable (diameter, 200 mm; N.A., 0.37; length, 1.5m; AniLab) attached to the implanted cannula
and connected to a blue LED (470 nm, power: 10mw-25mw, AniLab) under the control of a LED driver (AniLab). Mice were kept in
constant darkness (DD) or constant lightness (LL) and allowed to free run for at least 5 days before blue light stimulation. Blue light
pulses (470 nm, 4Hz,10mw-15mw, 10 ms duration, 1 h) were generated at various circadian times. Mice were excluded from analysis
if the ﬁber or virus was off-target by histology.
Body temperature (Tb) and locomotor activity (LMA) analyses
Mice were individually housed in cages with ad lib access to food and water. Data was collected using DSI (TA-F10 transmitter, Data
Sciences International, New Brighton, MN, USA). We calculated the phase response curve by identifying the changes in behavior (Tb
and LMA) onsets evoked by optogenetic stimulation compared to the same mouse under free-running conditions (DD and LL). We
identiﬁed activity onset and calculated phase shift compared to the same mouse under free-running conditions and allowed the in-
clusion of mice with visibly different periods. The phase shift is the difference between the predicted phase (the predicted value is
calculated by the free-running period of each mouse) and the observed phase.
SmFISH
Mice were transcardially perfused with 4% PFA (in 13 PBS) at different circadian times after circadian entrainment, as mentioned
above. The perfusion was performed quickly under dim red light. Brains were harvested and then immersed into 4% PFA kept at
4 C for >24h. The tissue was immersed in 10% sucrose in 13 PBS at 4 C. This step was repeated with 20% sucrose in 13 PBS,
followed by 30% sucrose in 13 PBS, each time allowing the tissue to sink to the bottom of the container. The brain was cut into a
2mm 3 2mm 3 2mm cube containing the SCN. The tissue was frozen in OCT with liquid nitrogen and stored at 80 C. Then the
tissue was cryosectioned (Leica CM1950) into 10-mm sections and stored in an airtight container at 80 C. Each SCN was sequen-
tially divided into 4 similar sets in order to perform different experiments. All sections were mounted with SuperFrost Plus slides
(Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc, catalog no. 4951PLUS). RNAscope in situ hybridizations were performed according to the manufacturer’s
instructions, using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent kit V2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) for ﬁxed frozen tissue. Reagent dosage
was adjusted according to the sample size. The slices were imaged with a confocal microscope at 320 magniﬁcation (Nikon A1).

CNO administration
Clozapine N-oxide (CNO; C0832, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in sterile saline to a concentration of 1mg/ml and kept as frozen
aliquots. Before each experiment, CNO was diluted with saline to 0.5mg/ml and administered by intraperitoneal injection to each
mouse (0.3 ml/30 g body weight) through the silicon tube. For light induced experiment, we inject CNO into mice at ZT 14 and ZT
22. For 6-hour phase advance jet lag experiment, we inject CNO into mice at ZT6 before light off during 6-hour phase advance jet lag.
Wheel running behavior analyses
Mice were individually housed in cages containing wireless running wheels with ad lib access to food and water. Data was collected
using Low-Proﬁle Wireless Running Wheel (Med Associates Inc). Mice were entrained to a 12 hours light (200-300lux) 12 hours dark
cycle for a minimum of 7 days prior to the release into DD, 20L:4D or LL (100lux). We used c2 periodogram to calculated free-running
period over 7 days for each mouse. We used ActogramJ software (imageJ plugin) to identify activity onset and offset from wheel
running data in 1-min bin.42 Animals with a loss of circadian rhythms were excluded from period calculations.
QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Statistical tests and sample sizes are provided in the ﬁgure legends. R (R Software, https://www.r-project.org) was used for statistical
analysis. For ﬁber photometry data analysis, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and two-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc test were
used to compare calcium signal waveforms. For wheel running data analysis, unpaired Student’s t test was used to compare period
and two-way ANOVA test was used to compare actogram proﬁle, onset, offset and alpha. For Tb and LMA data analysis, one-way
ANOVA and two-way ANOVA with Tukey or Scheffe HSD post hoc test were used to compare phase shift. Data are presented as the
mean ± SEM. Levels of statistical signiﬁcance are indicated as follows: n.s.: not signiﬁcant, *P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001, and
****P<0.0001.
